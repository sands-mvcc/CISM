Lesson Overview

Identify, Analyze, and Assess Information Security Risk

This lesson develops the learner's ability to identify, analyze, assess, and manage organizational information security risks in alignment with business objectives. The focus is not simply technical controls, but executive-level decision making, governance alignment, and operational risk reasoning.

Part 1 covers the diagnostic phase of risk management — knowing what you have, what threatens it, how badly it could be hurt, and how to measure it. Part 2 covers the response phase — deciding what to do about it.

Anchoring Analogy: Risk Management Is Like Medicine

A doctor doesn't prescribe treatment before diagnosing. They take a history (governance and scoping), check vital signs (assets and threats), order tests (vulnerability assessment), and develop a differential diagnosis (risk analysis) — then they treat. Part 1 is the diagnostic phase. Skip it, and the cure may be worse than the disease.

Learning Objectives

  1. Differentiate governance, risk management, and compliance responsibilities.
  2. Explain inherent risk, residual risk, risk appetite, tolerance, and capacity.
  3. Identify organizational assets, vulnerabilities, threats, and attack surfaces.
  4. Analyze how operational, compliance, technology, and reputational risks intersect.
  5. Compare qualitative, quantitative, and semi-quantitative risk assessments.
  6. Evaluate vulnerability severity using CVE and CVSS concepts.
  7. Explain penetration testing methodologies and red / blue / purple team operations.
  8. Recommend appropriate risk treatment strategies based on business requirements.
  9. Explain the importance of continuous baseline review and threat intelligence.
  10. Apply risk management concepts to realistic enterprise scenarios.
Module 1 45 min

Governance, Risk Management & Compliance (GRC)

GRC is often spoken as one acronym, but it represents three distinct organizational responsibilities. Confusing them is one of the most common — and most damaging — sources of governance failure.

The Three GRC Functions

Click each card to reveal what the function does and who owns it.

🏛️

Governance

Click to flip
Defines direction Sets risk appetite, strategic direction, and resource allocation. Owned by the Board and executive leadership. Answers: "What risks are we willing to take, and what are we trying to protect?"
⚙️

Risk Management

Click to flip
Executes controls Identifies, assesses, treats, and monitors risk. Owned by CISO, risk officers, and business unit leaders. Answers: "How do we reduce exposure to within the boundaries governance set?"
✅

Compliance

Click to flip
Validates adherence Confirms policies, laws, standards, and regulations are followed. Owned by Compliance, Internal Audit, and Legal. Answers: "Are we actually doing what we said — and what the regulator requires?"
Analogy: Building a House

Governance is the architect — deciding the blueprint, the budget, and what the house is for. Risk management is the construction crew — building walls, foundations, and roof. Compliance is the building inspector — verifying that what was built matches the blueprint and the code. Each role is essential; none can substitute for another.

Why Compliance Is Ineffective Without Governance

A compliance function can verify that the organization follows its policies — but it cannot decide whether those policies are the right ones. Without governance setting the strategic direction, compliance simply ensures the organization is consistently doing the wrong thing. Compliance is necessary; it is not sufficient.

Module 2 60 min

Foundational Risk Concepts

Five terms anchor every risk conversation. They sound similar, and even experienced practitioners mix them up. Master these and the rest of the lesson — and the rest of Domain 2 — becomes easier.

The Five Foundational Terms

🌋

Inherent Risk

Click to flip
Raw exposure before controls The risk that exists from the activity itself, without any protective measures. Example: hosting a public-facing web server has inherent risk of compromise.
🛡️

Residual Risk

Click to flip
What remains after controls The exposure left over once protections are applied. Never zero. The goal is to push it below tolerance.
🎯

Risk Appetite

Click to flip
Strategic willingness The aggregate amount of risk the organization is willing to pursue in support of its strategy. Set at the board level.
📏

Risk Tolerance

Click to flip
Operational variance Acceptable variation around appetite for a specific risk category. The boundary that triggers escalation when crossed.
⚠️

Risk Capacity

Click to flip
Maximum survivable The absolute limit of loss the organization can absorb without failing. Capacity is biology; appetite is choice.
Analogy: Driving on the Highway

Capacity is the maximum speed your car can physically reach before falling apart. Appetite is the speed you choose to drive based on your goals. Tolerance is how far above the speed limit you'll let the speedometer drift before tapping the brakes. Inherent risk is the danger of driving at all. Residual risk is what's left after seatbelts, airbags, and ABS.

Discussion Prompt

Can residual risk ever be zero? No — and the question itself reveals a misunderstanding. Eliminating residual risk would require either eliminating the asset (no risk if there's nothing to protect) or controls that are perfect, which don't exist. Mature risk programs target residual risk below appetite, not at zero.

Module 3 45 min

Risk Identification & Scoping

You cannot protect what you do not know exists. Risk identification is the systematic inventory of what we have, what could threaten it, and where the weaknesses are. Most organizations discover during their first thorough scoping exercise that they own significantly more attack surface than they realized.

Analogy: Insuring a House

Before an insurer writes a policy, they walk through the house, list the rooms, photograph the contents, note the fire exits, check the roof age, and identify the neighbors' fence height. Skip any of those, and the premium is wrong and the claim is contested. Cybersecurity scoping is no different — you cannot price or treat what you have not catalogued.

The Three Things to Identify

ElementWhat to CaptureCommon Misses
AssetsSystems, data, applications, people, processes, third-party servicesShadow IT, forgotten subdomains, expired SaaS trials still holding data
ThreatsAdversaries, hazards, error sources, environmental risksInsiders, supply chain, geopolitical disruption
VulnerabilitiesTechnical flaws, process gaps, training gaps, design weaknessesConfiguration drift, dormant accounts, undocumented integrations

Frameworks to Anchor the Work

FrameworkOriginBest Used For
NIST RMF (SP 800-37)U.S. National Institute of Standards and TechnologyFederal systems, defense contractors, regulated industries
NIST SP 800-30NISTDetailed risk assessment methodology
ISO 31000International Organization for StandardizationInternational, cross-industry enterprise risk management
CIS ControlsCenter for Internet SecurityPrioritized technical control implementation
ISACA Risk ITISACAIT risk in business context (CISM-aligned)
Key Teaching Point

Frameworks are guidance structures, not rigid checklists. Treating NIST or ISO as a compliance script — checking boxes without thinking — produces paperwork that satisfies auditors and protects nothing. Frameworks describe what to think about; judgment provides what to actually do.

External relationships that introduce risk through trust or integration.

Cloud-specific assets often missed in traditional asset inventories.

  • IaaS environments (AWS, Azure, GCP accounts and subscriptions)
  • SaaS applications storing regulated data
  • Cloud storage buckets (a chronic source of leaks)
  • API integrations and OAuth-granted access
  • Container registries and CI/CD pipelines
Module 4 45 min

Risk Categories & Business Impact

Cyber risk is rarely just "cyber" risk. A single technical incident cascades through strategic, operational, compliance, reputational, and HR domains. Mature risk managers categorize risk along all of these axes — not just one.

The Six Risk Categories

CategoryDefinitionExample
Strategic RiskThreats to long-term business objectives, market position, or competitive standingIP theft enabling a competitor to leapfrog product roadmap
Operational RiskDisruption of day-to-day business activities and processesRansomware halting production for 36 hours
Compliance RiskFailure to meet legal, regulatory, or contractual obligationsHIPAA breach notification triggering OCR investigation
Technology RiskFailure of systems, infrastructure, or technical controlsCloud provider regional outage taking down customer portal
Reputational RiskDamage to customer trust, brand value, or public standingPublic breach disclosure causing customer churn and stock drop
Human Resources RiskInsider misconduct, error, or loss of critical personnelDisgruntled administrator exfiltrating data before departure
Analogy: A Single Strike, Many Wounds

A car accident is not just "vehicle damage." It is also medical (injury), legal (liability), financial (insurance, lost wages), schedule (missed work and appointments), and emotional (trauma). A cyber incident is the same — one event, many simultaneous categories of harm.

The Data Breach Domino Effect

Click through the dominoes to see how a single phishing email cascades through every risk category.

🎣 Domino 1 — Initial Phishing Compromise ▶

Technology Risk. A finance employee clicks a phishing link. Credentials are harvested. The attacker authenticates as the employee and accesses email and SharePoint.

🏭 Domino 2 — Operational Disruption ▶

Operational Risk. The attacker pivots, deploys ransomware. ERP and order processing go offline. Production stops. Customer shipments are delayed. The incident response cost includes overtime, outside counsel, forensics, and lost productivity.

⚖️ Domino 3 — Regulatory Violation ▶

Compliance Risk. Customer PII was exposed. State breach notification laws trigger across 18 jurisdictions. If health data was involved, HIPAA Office for Civil Rights opens an investigation. Fines, audit costs, and consent decrees follow.

📰 Domino 4 — Customer Trust Damage ▶

Reputational Risk. Local news, then trade press, then national coverage. Customers churn. Sales cycles lengthen as prospects ask for SOC 2 reports and security questionnaires that didn't exist before. The brand damage outlasts the technical recovery by years.

💰 Domino 5 — Financial Consequences ▶

Strategic Risk. The cumulative impact — direct recovery, lost revenue, fines, legal costs, insurance premium increases, customer churn, and delayed product launches — materially affects the company's strategic position. Equifax spent over $1.4B on its 2017 breach; the original technical fix was a fraction.

Why Reputational Damage Often Exceeds Technical Damage

Technical recovery is measured in hours, days, or weeks. Reputational recovery is measured in years. Restoring a server costs what the vendor charges; restoring customer trust costs whatever the market demands — and you don't set that price.

Threat Actors & Attack Surfaces

Not all attackers are the same. A script kiddie scanning for low-hanging vulnerabilities does not require the same defense as a nation-state with custom malware and patience. Knowing who might attack — their motivation, capability, and patience — shapes how the defense is built.

The Five Adversary Types

👶

Script Kiddies

Click to flip
Low skill, opportunistic Use prebuilt tools, scan broadly for known vulnerabilities. Motivation: notoriety, curiosity. Basic hygiene defeats them.
💰

Cybercriminals

Click to flip
Profit-driven, organized Ransomware operators, fraud syndicates, data brokers. Motivation: money. Mature ecosystems with affiliates, support, and ransomware-as-a-service.
🚪

Insider Threats

Click to flip
Already trusted Disgruntled employees, careless users, or contractors. Motivation: revenge, money, ideology, or accident. Already inside the perimeter; hardest to detect.
🏢

Competitors

Click to flip
Industrial espionage Targeted theft of IP, pricing, or customer lists. Often work through proxies or hired contractors. Motivation: market advantage.
🌍

Nation-State / APT

Click to flip
Highly resourced, patient State-sponsored groups with custom tooling, zero-days, and multi-year operations. Motivation: geopolitics, espionage, disruption. Defense requires deep coordination with government and ISACs.
Analogy: Locks Are Sized to the Threat

A bicycle in a small town gets a basic lock; a bicycle in Manhattan gets a U-lock and a chain; a bicycle worth $10,000 gets brought inside. The same applies in security — the locks must match the adversary you're realistically facing, not a generic threat model. A small accounting firm doesn't need to defend like a defense contractor — but a defense contractor that defends like a small accounting firm becomes a national security incident.

Module 6 45 min

Threat Intelligence & Automation

Threat intelligence (TI) is information about adversaries, their tools, tactics, and intentions — refined into actionable form. Done well, it tells defenders what to watch for before the attack lands. Done poorly, it is a firehose of noise that wastes analyst time.

Analogy: Weather Reports vs. Lightning Strikes

Threat intel is the weather forecast — predictive, contextual, sometimes wrong. A SIEM alert is the lightning strike — immediate, concrete, requiring response. Both matter; they answer different questions. The forecast tells you whether to plan an outdoor wedding; the strike tells you to take cover now.

The Threat Intelligence Lifecycle

PhaseWhat HappensOutput
Planning & DirectionDefine intelligence requirements — what do we need to know?Priority Intelligence Requirements (PIRs)

ProcessingNormalize, deduplicate, translate, decodeStructured data in formats like STIX
AnalysisAdd context, assess relevance, evaluate confidenceFinished intelligence products
DisseminationPush to the people and systems that can use itSIEM rules, blocklists, executive briefings
FeedbackDid it help? Refine requirements based on resultsImproved PIRs for the next cycle

H-ISAC, MS-ISAC, E-ISACISACHealthcare, state/local government, energy sectors

STIX & TAXII — The Sharing Standards

STIX (Structured Threat Information Expression) is the language — a standardized format for describing threats. TAXII (Trusted Automated Exchange of Intelligence Information) is the delivery mechanism — the protocol for moving STIX data between organizations. Together they enable automation: a CISA advisory can flow into a corporate SIEM without manual rekeying.

Vulnerability Management & Baselines

A vulnerability is a weakness that, if exploited, could lead to a security breach. Vulnerability management is the continuous cycle of discovering, assessing, prioritizing, and remediating those weaknesses. It is one of the highest-leverage activities a security program does — and one of the most commonly mismanaged.

CVE and CVSS — The Common Language

AcronymStands ForWhat It Does
CVECommon Vulnerabilities and ExposuresUnique identifier for a specific vulnerability (e.g., CVE-2024-12345)
CVSSCommon Vulnerability Scoring SystemNumerical 0–10 severity score across multiple dimensions
NVDNational Vulnerability DatabaseNIST-maintained repository correlating CVEs with CVSS scores
KEVKnown Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogCISA's list of CVEs actively used in attacks — always prioritize these

High Severity ≠ High Organizational Risk

A CVSS 9.8 vulnerability on an isolated air-gapped lab system is less organizational risk than a CVSS 7.0 on an internet-facing customer portal handling payment data. Scanner output requires human interpretation. The score tells you the technical severity; only you know the business context.

Credentialed vs. Non-Credentialed Scans

Scan TypeHow It WorksBest Used For
Non-CredentialedExternal view — what an unauthenticated attacker would seeAttack surface discovery, internet-facing assessment
CredentialedLogs in to the system — sees installed software, patches, configurationsCompliance, configuration drift, comprehensive vulnerability inventory
Agent-BasedAgent installed on the system reports continuouslyMobile workforce, cloud-native environments, real-time visibility

Baseline Configuration Management

A baseline is the documented "known good" configuration for each system class. Configuration drift — gradual deviation from baseline through ad-hoc changes — is one of the most common sources of vulnerability. Continuous baseline review catches drift before it becomes a breach.

Module 8 45 min

Penetration Testing & Security Teams

Vulnerability scanning tells you what might be exploitable. Penetration testing tells you what is exploitable, by simulating the attacker. The two are complementary, not substitutes.

Three Testing Methodologies

⬜

White-Box

Click to flip
Full knowledge Tester has source code, architecture diagrams, credentials, and design documents. Deepest assessment; finds design-level flaws. Most efficient for thorough review.
▣

Gray-Box

Click to flip
Partial knowledge Tester has limited insider information — typical user credentials, basic architecture. Simulates a compromised user or malicious insider. Balanced realism and depth.
⬛

Black-Box

Click to flip
No knowledge Tester has only what's publicly visible. Simulates an external attacker starting from zero. Most realistic; slowest; may miss internal flaws.
Analogy: Hiring a Burglar to Test Your House

Black-box is hiring a burglar who knows only the street address. Gray-box is hiring one who has the layout but not the alarm codes. White-box is hiring one who has the blueprints, the alarm codes, and the security camera placements. Each finds different weaknesses.

The Attack Chain (MITRE ATT&CK Phases)

Pen testers and real attackers follow a similar progression. Click each phase.

🔍 1 · Reconnaissance ▶

Open-source intelligence (OSINT) gathering: employee names from LinkedIn, technology stack from job postings, exposed services from Shodan. No interaction with the target yet.

💥 2 · Exploitation ▶

Initial access — exploiting a vulnerability, sending a phishing email, or using stolen credentials. The first foothold on the network.

⬆️ 3 · Privilege Escalation ▶

Moving from a low-privilege user account to administrative or system-level access. Frequently uses misconfigurations, unpatched local vulnerabilities, or harvested credentials.

🪤 4 · Persistence ▶

Establishing a way to maintain access even after reboots or password changes — scheduled tasks, backdoor accounts, malicious services, or implants.

↔️ 5 · Lateral Movement ▶

Pivoting from the initial foothold to other systems — file servers, domain controllers, databases, executive workstations. This is where most damage happens.

🎯 6 · Objective / Actions on Target ▶

Achieving the attacker's actual goal — data exfiltration, ransomware deployment, financial fraud, sabotage, or long-term espionage.

Red, Blue, and Purple Teams

TeamRoleMindset
Red TeamOffensive — simulate adversariesGoal-oriented, creative, exploits assumptions
Blue TeamDefensive — detect and respondPattern-recognition, telemetry, incident response
Purple TeamCollaborative — Red and Blue working togetherIterative improvement, knowledge transfer, exercise-driven
White TeamObservers / refereesGovern the exercise, ensure scope, score results
Why Purple Teams Outperform Pure Red-vs-Blue

An adversarial Red team that wins and disappears teaches defenders nothing. A collaborative Purple team that says "we got in this way — here is the telemetry you should have seen" creates lasting defensive improvement. The win condition is not embarrassment; it is detection capability.

Risk Assessment Methodologies

There are three families of risk assessment. None is universally best; each fits different organizational contexts.

The Three Methodology Families

MethodologyHow It WorksBest ForLimitation
QualitativeLikelihood and impact rated High / Medium / LowSpeed, executive communication, small organizationsSubjective; hard to compare across teams
QuantitativeLikelihood and impact in numeric dollars and probabilitiesInsurance, large enterprise, financial industriesRequires data; precision can mislead if inputs are guesses
Semi-QuantitativeNumeric scales (1–5 or 1–10) with structured criteriaMost mid-size organizations; balanced approachStill some subjectivity in rating definitions
Analogy: Estimating Travel Time

Qualitative: "The trip will take a long time." Quantitative: "GPS estimates 2 hours, 14 minutes via the highway." Semi-quantitative: "Plan for 2 to 3 hours depending on traffic." Each is right for different audiences and decisions. The board wants qualitative; the project manager wants semi-quantitative; the insurance underwriter wants quantitative.

The Core Quantitative Formula

TermFormulaMeaning
Asset Value (AV)Replacement or business valueWhat the asset is worth to the organization
Exposure Factor (EF)% of asset value lost in one incident (0.0–1.0)How much harm a single incident causes
Single Loss Expectancy (SLE)AV × EFExpected loss per incident
Annualized Rate of Occurrence (ARO)Incidents per yearHow frequently it's expected to happen
Annualized Loss Expectancy (ALE)SLE × AROExpected annual loss

Module 10 45 min

Risk Treatment & Response

Once a risk has been identified, scoped, categorized, and assessed, the organization must decide what to do. The four treatment strategies are introduced here and explored in depth in Part 2 of this domain.

The Four Treatment Strategies (Preview)

🛡️

Mitigation

Click to flip
Reduce Apply controls to lower likelihood or impact. The most common response. Example: deploying MFA reduces credential-stuffing risk.
📤

Transfer

Click to flip
Shift Move the financial impact to a third party — typically cyber insurance or contractual indemnification. Note: accountability does not transfer with money.
🚫

Avoidance

Click to flip
Eliminate Don't engage in the risky activity. Example: decline to operate in a sanctioned country, or retire an unfixable legacy system rather than continue running it.
✅

Acceptance

Click to flip
Tolerate (formally) Acknowledge and document. Acceptance must be signed, dated, time-bound, and supported by analysis. Never the default — it must be deliberate.
Key Learning Point

Risk decisions are business decisions, not purely technical decisions. The CISO recommends; the business owner decides. The technical team can describe the vulnerability and the cost of treatment, but the choice to accept, mitigate, transfer, or avoid belongs to the executive who owns the business outcome.

Case Study: The Legacy Manufacturing System

Scenario: A 12-year-old manufacturing control system supports critical production for a $400M revenue line. It cannot be patched — the vendor is bankrupt. Replacement would cost $4.5M and take 18 months. Cyber insurance is available but excludes ICS coverage. The system is reachable from the corporate network. A high-severity vulnerability has just been disclosed.

Which response is most defensible?
Acceptance — the cost of replacement is too high
Avoidance — shut down the production line until replaced
Mitigation — segment the network, monitor heavily, plan the replacement
Transfer — buy a cyber insurance policy and continue operating

Required Deliverables
