Lesson Overview

From Governance to the Operating Floor

Part 1 of Domain 4 covered the strategic side of incident management — preparation, governance, communication, and lifecycle theory. Part 2 takes you onto the operating floor. This is where SOC analysts triage alerts at 3 AM, where EDR consoles isolate hosts, where forensic investigators image disks under the watchful eye of legal counsel, and where vendor failures expose the brutal truth that you cannot outsource accountability.

Anchoring Analogy: The Surgery, Not the Hospital

If Part 1 was the hospital — accreditation, staffing models, protocols, training curricula — then Part 2 is the surgery itself. The scrub procedures, the instrument tray, the chain of evidence in the operating room, the post-op review, the malpractice exposure when something goes wrong. Both matter; this is the half where lives are saved or lost.

Module 1 60 min

Security Operations & Automation

A modern enterprise generates tens of millions of log events per day. Firewalls, endpoints, identity providers, cloud control planes, applications, network sensors — all producing telemetry around the clock. No human team can read it manually. The Security Operations Center (SOC) and its Security Information and Event Management (SIEM) platform exist to turn that flood into actionable signal.

Analogy: The Air Traffic Control Tower

An air traffic controller doesn't watch every plane on every screen at every moment. The radar system flags conflicts — two aircraft converging, an altitude deviation, a frequency dropout — and the controller decides which to act on. The radar is the SIEM. The controller is the analyst. Automation surfaces the signal; humans make the call. Take away the radar and the airspace becomes uncontrollable. Take away the controller and the radar is just blinking lights.

Why Manual SOCs Fail

ProblemWhy It Breaks Manual Operations
VolumeAn average mid-size enterprise generates 5–20 million log events daily. Reading them is mathematically impossible.
HeterogeneityLogs from 40+ sources in different formats, time zones, and severity scales. Without normalization, correlation is guesswork.
VelocityAn attacker can move laterally in minutes. A SOC that needs an hour to assemble a picture is already behind.
Alert fatigueIf 95% of alerts are false positives, analysts begin to ignore the 5% that matter. This is how breaches go undetected for 200+ days.

What Automation Actually Does

  • Normalization — translate every log source into a common schema so events from a firewall and a Windows server can be compared
  • Correlation — connect events across sources: "failed login at the VPN followed by a privilege escalation on a domain controller is one story, not two events"
  • Enrichment — add threat intel, asset criticality, user context to raw events so analysts get a complete picture, not a fragment
  • Prioritization — score alerts by likely impact so analysts work the worst cases first
  • SOAR (orchestration) — execute repeatable response actions automatically: isolate the host, block the IP, disable the account

Instructor Emphasis

Continuous SIEM tuning is mandatory. A SIEM deployed with vendor-default rules and never touched again becomes a noise machine within months. Tuning is a job, not a project — every false positive that survives is a vote for analysts to ignore the next alert. Budget for at least one full-time engineer on use case and detection content development.

Module 2 60 min

Detection & Response Technologies

Three closely-related acronyms get confused constantly: EDR, XDR, and MDR. They are not competitors — they describe different scopes and different operating models. Choosing among them is one of the most consequential security architecture decisions an organization makes.

The Three Technologies

💻

EDR

Endpoint Detection & Response
Focus: endpoint telemetry Monitors processes, file changes, network connections, and registry activity on individual hosts. Can isolate machines, terminate processes, and roll back changes. The microscope.
🌐

XDR

Extended Detection & Response
Focus: cross-platform telemetry Correlates endpoint, network, cloud, email, and identity events into unified timelines. ML-based detection across the stack. The control tower.
👥

MDR

Managed Detection & Response
Focus: outsourced operations A vendor provides 24×7 monitoring, threat hunting, and incident response — typically built on top of EDR or XDR tooling. Renting the SOC.
Analogy: Three Ways to Watch a House

EDR is putting motion sensors on every window and door — detailed coverage of each entry point, but each sensor reports independently. XDR is adding a central security panel that combines all sensors, the doorbell camera, and the neighborhood watch feed into one timeline. MDR is hiring a monitoring company that watches the panel for you and dispatches help when something happens. You still own the house, the keys, and the liability.

Outsourcing Does NOT Transfer Accountability

An MDR contract transfers operations. It does not transfer the regulator's letter, the lawsuit, the customer churn, or the board's questions. When an MDR provider misses a ransomware detection at 2 AM and the customer wakes up to encrypted servers at 6 AM, the headline reads "Company X Hit by Ransomware," not "Vendor Y Failed to Detect." The contract is the floor, not the ceiling.

People, Team Structures & Skills

The most expensive SIEM in the world produces nothing without the team that operates it. SOC and IR teams come in three structural patterns, each with strengths and failure modes.

Three SOC Structures

🏢

Centralized SOC

Click to flip
Single team, single location Faster coordination, easier governance, simpler tooling. Risk: regional blind spots, single point of failure, limited time-zone coverage without overnight shifts.
🌍

Distributed SOC

Click to flip
Multiple regional teams Follow-the-sun coverage, regional expertise, geographic redundancy. Risk: coordination complexity, handoff gaps, inconsistent procedures across sites.
🤝

Outsourced SOC

Click to flip
Vendor-operated Predictable cost, instant 24×7 coverage, deep specialty talent. Risk: vendor dependency, contextual blindness, escalation friction at the worst moments.
Analogy: City vs. County vs. Contract Fire Service

A centralized SOC is the city fire department — one station, one chief, everyone trained the same way. A distributed SOC is a county system — multiple stations covering different areas, shared standards, coordinated dispatch. An outsourced SOC is a contracted fire service — a company hired to provide coverage, with a contract specifying response times. Each model works; each fails differently.

Soft Skills Are Operationally Critical

It is tempting to hire IR talent purely on technical credentials. Do not. A brilliant analyst who cannot brief an executive under pressure, write a clear ticket, or stay calm at 3 AM will be a liability at exactly the wrong moment. The skills that matter most when the stakes are highest are: communication, leadership under stress, integrity, and the ability to say "I don't know yet" without panicking.

Step 1 — Who owns the incident at the executive level? ▶

CISO or VP of IT/Security — accountable to the Board. Has authority to declare a major incident, approve shutdowns, and approve external engagement.

Step 2 — Who coordinates the response in the moment? ▶

IR Manager (incident commander) — the scene leader. Runs the war room, manages the cadence, makes operational calls. Reports to CISO.

Step 3 — Who does the technical work? ▶

Lead Analyst + 1–2 SOC analysts + IT engineer. Forensics is often hybrid — a retainer with a specialist firm for deep investigation, especially for litigation-sensitive incidents.

Step 4 — Who handles the non-technical fallout? ▶

Legal counsel (engaged pre-incident, on retainer), Communications lead, HR partner, Compliance officer. All four are core team members — not "called in if needed."

Step 5 — Who covers nights and weekends? ▶

On-call rotation with paging, plus an MDR vendor for after-hours triage. A 24×7 internal SOC for a hospital of this size is rarely economical; hybrid models are standard.

Module 4 90 min

Identification, Investigation & Digital Forensics

Forensics is the discipline that turns chaos into evidence — and evidence into something that holds up in court. Done correctly, it determines what happened, how, and (sometimes) by whom. Done sloppily, it produces conclusions a court will throw out and an attacker the organization can never prosecute.

The Critical Distinction: Event vs. Incident

📡 Event

Any observable occurrence in a system or network.

  • A user logs in
  • A firewall blocks a packet
  • A backup completes
  • A patch installs

Events are the raw material. Most events are routine.

🚨 Incident

An event (or series of events) that requires formal response due to business impact or security implications.

  • Confirmed malware infection
  • Unauthorized data access
  • Successful phishing leading to credential theft
  • Service outage with regulatory exposure

Incidents are events that demand action and decisions.

Analogy: Smoke Alarms and House Fires

A smoke alarm chirping because someone burned toast is an event. The same alarm pattern combined with visible flames and the smell of plastic is an incident. The alarm hardware doesn't know the difference — humans do. SIEM alerts work the same way: every alert is an event; only some are incidents.

E-Discovery vs. Digital Forensics

DisciplinePurposeScope
E-DiscoveryIdentify and produce electronically stored information (ESI) for litigationBroad — emails, documents, chat logs, databases. Quantity over depth.
Digital ForensicsScientifically collect, preserve, and analyze evidence of what happenedDeep — disk images, memory captures, log timelines, network packet captures. Depth over quantity.

Chain of Custody — The Legal Backbone

Chain of custody is the documented trail showing who handled a piece of evidence, when, where, and why — from the moment of collection to its presentation in court. Break the chain, and the evidence is usually inadmissible. This is the rule that makes everything else in forensics matter.

Analogy: The Witness Stand

Imagine a witness testifying that they saw something happen. The judge asks: "Where were you? Who else was there? How did you get there? What did you do next?" If the witness can't answer coherently, the testimony is dismissed. Chain of custody is the evidence's testimony. If you can't say who had this evidence at every moment between the incident and now, the court will not let it speak.

The Order of Volatility

When investigating, capture evidence in order from most volatile to least volatile. Volatile data disappears with reboot, time, or routine system activity. Get it first — or lose it forever.

  1. CPU registers and cache Highest volatility
    Gone in nanoseconds. Captured only with specialized memory acquisition tools.
  2. RAM (live memory) Highest volatility
    Gone at reboot. Contains running processes, decrypted secrets, network connections, malware artifacts that never touch disk.
  3. Medium volatility Network state — active connections, ARP tables, routing tables
    Changes second to second. Capture before disconnecting from network.
  4. Medium volatility Running processes and open files
    Lost at shutdown. Document before any containment action.
  5. Medium volatility Disk contents — file systems, slack space, unallocated areas
    Persistent but writable. Capture via forensic image with write-blocker.
  6. Lower volatility Remote logs (SIEM, cloud) and archived backups
    Generally durable. Snapshot to prevent retention rotation from destroying them.
  7. Lower volatility Physical media — DVDs, optical archives, printed records
    Most durable. Capture last unless it is the only copy of relevant evidence.
The First Responder Trap

The instinct of a panicked first responder is to reboot the machine. Don't. A reboot destroys the most valuable evidence you'll ever have access to — live memory containing the malware in execution, attacker tooling in flight, decrypted credentials, and command-and-control session state. Capture memory first. Reboot last, if at all.

Module 5 75 min

Containment, Eradication & Recovery

Three operational phases, three distinct missions. Confusing them — or compressing them — produces the most common failure mode in IR: declaring victory too soon and getting reinfected within days.

The Three Operational Phases

🚧

Containment

Click to flip
Stop the bleeding Isolate affected systems, reset compromised credentials, update firewalls, quarantine malicious files. Goal: limit blast radius while preserving evidence.
🧹

Eradication

Click to flip
Remove the threat completely Remove rootkits, patch vulnerabilities, eliminate persistence mechanisms, rotate any potentially compromised credentials. Verify the environment is clean before recovery.
♻️

Recovery

Click to flip
Return to normal — carefully Restore from trusted backups, validate system integrity, monitor for reintroduction. Sequence restoration by business priority per the BIA.
Analogy: A Termite Infestation

Containment is sealing off the affected rooms so the termites can't spread. Eradication is the pest-control treatment that kills the colony and finds the queen. Recovery is rebuilding the chewed wood and moving the family back in. Try to skip eradication and move family back in after containment, and the termites are right back — only now you don't notice them until the floor collapses.

Containment Strategies

TypeActionTrade-off
Short-term containmentNetwork isolation via EDR, firewall block, account disableFast but rough — may alert the attacker or destroy in-flight evidence
System backupForensic image of affected systems before any cleanupRequired for both legal evidence and post-incident learning
Long-term containmentTemporary patches, monitored rebuilds, controlled re-enablementMethodical but slower — buys time for proper eradication

Why Eradication Cannot Be Skipped

The single most damaging IR mistake is restoring systems before the threat is fully eradicated. Variations of this story repeat every week in incident reports:

  • Team restores from a backup taken after the initial compromise — backup is already infected.
  • Team rebuilds the original system but reuses the same compromised credentials — attacker walks right back in.
  • Team eliminates the malware but misses the persistence mechanism (scheduled task, registry run key, malicious service) — malware reinstalls on next boot.
  • Team focuses on the obvious infected host and misses lateral movement — attacker is already on the file server.
Recovery Without Root-Cause Analysis Risks Reinfection

If you do not know how the attacker got in, you cannot prove they are out. Eradication requires identifying the initial vector, every persistence mechanism, every compromised credential, and every lateral movement path — and verifying each is closed before recovery begins. Anything less is hope, not response.

Scenario: Enterprise Ransomware — Sequencing the Response

A mid-sized manufacturer detects ransomware encrypting files on a finance department fileshare at 3:47 AM. EDR shows the encryption originated from a workstation, HOST-FIN-08.

What is the CORRECT first action?
Immediately restore the encrypted fileshare from last night's backup
Reboot HOST-FIN-08 to disrupt the malware
Isolate HOST-FIN-08 via EDR (network quarantine, keep it running), then capture memory before any cleanup
Notify the regulator within the first 30 minutes
Module 6 60 min

Incident Communication & Notification

If Part 1 established that communication failures often cause more damage than technical failures, this module is the operational follow-through. During an active incident, every audience needs a different message at a different cadence — and at least one channel of communication you control must be assumed compromised.

Out-of-Band Communication — The Channel That Survives

Analogy: The Phone in the Locked Drawer

If the attacker is reading your email — and during many breaches, they are — then every IR plan you email, every status update you send, and every credential reset announcement you broadcast is being read by both teams. Mature IR programs maintain an out-of-band communication channel: a separate platform (Signal, dedicated phone tree, separate cloud tenant) used only during incidents. The phone in the locked drawer that the attacker doesn't have a key to.

The Single Source of Truth

During a serious incident, you will have a dozen people simultaneously reading different versions of reality from different channels. The cure is a single, named, authoritative status document — updated on a fixed cadence by a named individual — that everyone references and no one duplicates. Without it, by hour three the executives, the engineers, and the call center will be working from incompatible facts.

The Three Audiences and Their Briefings

🏛️ Executive Briefing

"At 03:47 EDR detected ransomware on a finance workstation. Host isolated within 4 minutes. No lateral movement detected. ~40 GB of finance fileshare encrypted; data restorable from immutable backup. Expected impact: $200K in lost productivity, no customer data exposure. Next update at 9 AM."

  • Business impact in dollars and time
  • Customer/regulatory exposure
  • Decisions awaiting executive input
  • Next-update commitment
⚙️ Technical Operations

"Variant: LockBit 3.0 affiliate build, sha256 d4f8…. C2 to 185.220.101.45 blocked. Initial vector: phishing via DocuSign lure opened 02:15. Confirmed persistence via scheduled task SCHTASK_RANSOM-7. Memory image captured by 04:22. Need: validation that 03:00 backup snapshot pre-dates compromise."

  • Indicators of compromise (IOCs)
  • Specific procedures and tasks
  • Decisions awaiting technical input
  • Dependencies between activities
📣 Public / Customer Statement

"We detected a security incident affecting an internal finance system early this morning. The affected system has been isolated and we are investigating. At this time we have no indication that customer data was accessed. We will provide updates as our investigation progresses. Customers with questions may contact support@example.com."

  • Acknowledge the incident — don't deny what you can't prove
  • State what is known, what is being investigated, and what is not yet known
  • Provide a path for affected parties to get more information
  • Commit to updates without committing to a fixed timeline

Coordinate Law Enforcement Engagement With Legal

Engaging the FBI or other law enforcement is sometimes the right move — but never a unilateral decision by the IR team. Law enforcement involvement changes evidence-handling requirements, disclosure constraints, and litigation posture. The call goes through Legal Counsel, who weighs prosecution potential, regulatory implications, and attorney-client privilege before recommending to executive leadership.

Module 7 45 min

Post-Incident Review & Continuous Improvement

The Post-Incident Review (PIR) — sometimes called the after-action review, retrospective, or "lessons learned" — is where mature programs distinguish themselves. Done well, it produces concrete improvements that prevent the next incident. Done badly, it produces blame, defensive postures, and a team that learns to hide what really happened.

Analogy: The NTSB, Not the Inquisition

When an airliner crashes, the National Transportation Safety Board investigates not to punish but to learn. Crew members are interviewed under explicit non-punitive protections so they tell the truth about what they did and why. The resulting findings change aviation safety for everyone, forever. A PIR conducted as an inquisition produces lies and turnover; a PIR conducted as an NTSB-style investigation produces a stronger organization.

The Four Core PIR Activities

📅 Timeline Documentation

Reconstruct the incident minute by minute — what was detected when, what action was taken when, who made which decision. The factual record.

🗣️ Stakeholder Feedback

Hear from every party involved — technical responders, executives, business owners, vendors. What did they need that they didn't get?

🔬 Root Cause Analysis

Why did this happen? Not just the technical vector, but the process, people, and decisions that made it possible. The "five whys" applied to organizational failure.

💰 Budget & Policy Recommendations

What investments, tooling, training, or policy changes would have prevented this — or detected it sooner? Concrete asks tied to evidence.

The Three PIR Questions

Run every PIR through these three questions. They produce useful output reliably; almost anything else produces blame.

  1. What worked? — name the people, tools, decisions, and processes that performed well. Reinforce them.
  2. What failed? — name the gaps in capability, communication, or decision-making. Without naming, they cannot be fixed.
  3. What do we change? — for every "failed" finding, propose a specific action: a control, a training, a tool, a policy, a contract change.
PIR Is Not Blame-Focused — It Is Improvement-Focused

When the PIR finds that an analyst missed an alert, the question is not "why did Tier-1 fail?" — it is "why was this alert detectable only by manual review?" When the PIR finds that an executive made the wrong call, the question is not "why did the CFO panic?" — it is "why did the CFO not have the information needed to make a better call?" The same finding produces a firing in a blame culture and a control improvement in a learning culture. Choose deliberately.

The 30/60/90 Pattern

Mature programs run PIRs on a predictable schedule after every major incident:

  • Hot wash within 24–48 hours — quick debrief while memory is fresh. Capture the timeline; identify immediate fixes.
  • Formal PIR at 30 days — full investigation with stakeholder interviews, RCA, and recommendations.
  • Follow-up at 90 days — were the recommendations implemented? What residual exposure remains?

Organizations that stop at the hot wash never institutionalize the learning. Organizations that file the PIR report and never revisit it discover, 18 months later, that nothing actually changed.

Module 8 45 min

Accountability, Outsourcing & Governance

The most expensive lesson many organizations learn after a major incident is the same one: operational tasks can be outsourced; accountability cannot. The MDR provider's missed alert becomes your breach notification. The cloud provider's misconfigured default becomes your data leak. The forensic vendor's broken chain of custody becomes your inadmissible evidence.

📤

What You CAN Transfer

The day-to-day operation: monitoring, triage, alert handling, even initial response. Specialized capabilities like forensics, dark-web monitoring, threat hunting. Financial loss via cyber insurance.

⚖️

What You CANNOT Transfer

Regulatory accountability. Fiduciary duty to shareholders. The duty of care to customers. The headline. The lawsuit. The board's questions. The cost of brand damage. The decision authority over your own incident.

Analogy: Hiring a Property Manager

Hiring a property manager for a rental house transfers the operational headache — tenant calls, lawn care, maintenance scheduling. It does not transfer the mortgage, the property tax, the homeowner's liability, or the legal responsibility to maintain a habitable dwelling. If the property manager misses a code violation, the city cites the owner, not the manager. The contract may give the owner recourse against the manager — but the owner answers to the city first.

What a Defensible MDR Contract Specifies

  • SLA on detection time — minutes from log ingestion to alert, by severity
  • SLA on escalation time — from analyst triage to customer notification, by severity
  • Data ownership and portability — who owns the logs, alerts, and case data; how is it returned at contract end
  • Evidence handling — chain of custody procedures, forensic preservation, court-admissibility commitments
  • Escalation paths — named contacts on both sides, including after-hours and weekends
  • Incident response support — what the vendor will do when something fires, not just what they will detect
  • Audit rights — your ability to review their performance and procedures
  • Termination provisions — what happens to your data if you switch vendors or in-source
  • Indemnification limits — what the vendor pays if they fail to perform (almost always less than your actual loss)

Scenario: The MDR That Didn't Escalate

At 2:14 AM Sunday, your MDR provider's system detected unusual encryption activity on three corporate file servers. Their analyst reviewed the alert, classified it as "user-initiated bulk operation," and closed it without escalation. By Monday morning, 4.2 TB of customer data was encrypted and a ransom note was on every desktop.

When the SEC, state regulators, and customers ask "who is responsible for this breach?", the answer is:
The MDR vendor — their analyst made the wrong call
The organization — accountability cannot be outsourced
The cyber insurance carrier — that's what the policy is for
The CISO personally — they chose the vendor
Governance Oversight of Vendors Is Continuous

Signing the contract is the start, not the end. Mature programs review MDR performance against SLAs quarterly, conduct annual purple-team-style tests of vendor detection capability, audit a sample of their alert handling decisions, and treat vendor governance as a named program owned by a named person — not a side task for the procurement team.

Enterprise SOC Incident Simulation

Scenario: GlobalOps Manufacturing — Multi-Stage Compromise

You are the IR Manager at GlobalOps, a multinational manufacturer with $3B revenue, 12,000 employees, operations on three continents, and a hybrid cloud environment. Over 72 hours, the following timeline unfolds:

  • Day -14 (discovered Day 0): A finance director's M365 credentials were stolen via a phishing campaign. The attacker accessed her mailbox via OAuth grant, established mail forwarding rules, and began reconnaissance.
  • Day -7: The attacker pivoted to AWS via federated identity, harvested IAM keys, and provisioned persistence (a new IAM user with admin privileges, named to blend in with legitimate accounts).
  • Day -2: The attacker exfiltrated approximately 80 GB of customer pricing, supply chain contracts, and HR data via legitimate AWS APIs.
  • Day 0, 04:14: The attacker deployed ransomware via the AWS persistence to encrypt EC2-hosted ERP instances. Your MDR provider detected the encryption activity but classified it as "scheduled maintenance" and did not escalate.
  • Day 0, 07:30: Plant operations report ERP outage. You declare an incident.
